01

The measurement chain

Every screen in this module is one link in a single chain. Understanding the chain makes the rest of the module obvious; missing it makes each screen look like an unrelated table.

Framework→Indicator→Data record→Quality assurance→Report
FrameworkStates what change we are trying to cause, and the logic by which our activities are supposed to cause it. Built once per programme or project.
IndicatorThe specific thing we will count to know whether that change is happening. Hangs off a framework, and off a point in a project.
Data recordOne indicator's actual value for one reporting period, with the evidence behind it and an explanation if it missed.
Quality assuranceSomebody other than the person who entered it confirms the number is supported by the evidence.
ReportVerified numbers assembled for a donor, for management, for the M&E function, or for a formal impact evaluation.
Why the order is fixed

An indicator that does not trace to a framework is a number nobody agreed was worth collecting. A report built from unverified records is a claim with nothing behind it. The chain is the audit path a donor follows in reverse: from a figure in a report, to the record, to the evidence file, to the indicator definition, to the framework that justified measuring it.

02

MEL frameworks

MEL Frameworks

A framework is the top of the chain. The register lists each one with its ID, name, type, the programme or project it belongs to, who created it, when, and a status of Active, Draft or Archived. It filters by type and by creation date, and exports to CSV.

Building one

The builder captures the framework's identity, then its logic.

FieldNotes

DescriptionWhat the framework is for
Applicable levelInstitutional · Program Level · Project Level
Linked entityAppears only when the level is Program or Project — an institutional framework has nothing to link to, so the field does not exist for it
Start and end dateThe period the framework covers

The hierarchy builder

Beneath the header sits the results chain itself, built as levels containing items, where each item names its parent on the level above. The default is the standard four:

Goal / ImpactThe long-term change — "reduce youth unemployment by 20%"
OutcomeWhat must become true for the goal to follow — "youth acquire market-relevant digital skills", "employers increase hiring of trained youth"
OutputWhat our work directly produces — "digital training hubs established", "certification programme launched"
ActivityWhat we actually do — "renovate hub facilities", "procure equipment", "develop curriculum", "sign MoUs with employers"

Levels can be added, renamed and removed, so a framework that needs five tiers or different vocabulary is not forced into the default. Items link upward by parent, which is what makes the chain traceable in both directions: from an activity up to the goal it serves, and from a goal down to everything being done in its name.

The builder ends with a review step before saving, so the whole structure is confirmed as one piece rather than committed level by level.

03

Which framework type to use

All four types describe how activities lead to change, and they are often used loosely as synonyms. They answer different questions, and picking the wrong one produces a document nobody uses.

TypeAnswersShapeReach for it when
Theory of Change Why do we believe this will work? A causal map, often with several pathways, showing assumptions and the conditions each step depends on Designing a programme, or having to defend the logic to a sceptical board or donor. It is the only one of the four that makes assumptions explicit.
Results Framework What are we accountable for delivering? A strict hierarchy of results with indicators attached at each level Reporting performance upward. It is the performance instrument — trimmed of narrative, built for measurement.
Logical Framework How will this be managed and verified? A matrix: results down the side; indicator, means of verification, and assumptions across the top Managing delivery and satisfying a donor who requires a logframe. It is the structural instrument — every cell must be filled.
Logic Model What goes in, what comes out? A linear flow: inputs → activities → outputs → outcomes Explaining a project on one page to a general audience. The simplest of the four, and the least useful for measurement.
Structure versus performance, in one line

A logical framework is structural: it fixes the design, the means of verification and the assumptions, and is used to manage the work. A results framework is performance-oriented: it strips the design detail and keeps the results hierarchy with its indicators and targets, and is used to report the work. Many organisations maintain both — the logframe for delivery, the results framework for the donor — and the two must agree on the results they name.

04

Performance indicators

Performance Indicators

The indicator register is the working list of everything being measured. Each row carries the indicator ID, its indicator level, name, project, project level, unit of measure, data source, reporting frequency, target, actual, and a status of On Track, At Risk or Behind. It filters by unit, by level and by period, searches by name or ID, and exports to CSV.

Defining an indicator

The form is deliberately long, in four sections, because a badly defined indicator produces uncomparable data for years.

SectionCaptures
1 · Basic informationIndicator name and its precise definition, plus the Assignment card — where in the project this is tracked and what it is attached to
2 · Classification & strategyIndicator level, project, the framework it belongs to, and the specific outcome within that framework it reports against
3 · Measurement rules (PIRS)Unit of measure, data source, reporting frequency, and the Performance Indicator Reference Sheet itself — uploaded or linked
4 · BenchmarksBaseline value, target value, start date and target date
What the PIRS is for

A Performance Indicator Reference Sheet is the definitive statement of how an indicator is measured: precisely what counts, who collects it, how often, from which source, and how it is calculated. It exists so that the number means the same thing in year three as it did in year one, when the people who designed it have moved on. Section 3 is where that document is attached to the indicator rather than living in someone's folder.

The baseline deserves the same emphasis. Without a value recorded before the work started, an actual is a number with nothing to compare it to — the target tells you where you wanted to get to, the baseline tells you how far you have come.

05

The two "levels", untangled

The indicator table has two columns with "level" in the name, and they are genuinely different things. This is the single most common point of confusion in the module.

Indicator LevelProject Level
AnswersWhose indicator is this — who needs the number?Where in the project is it measured?
ValuesProject Level Indicator (Donor Requirement) · Program Level Indicator · Institutional KPIProject · Phase · Deliverable · Task
Set onThe Classification & Strategy sectionThe Assignment card, at the top of the form
DrivesWhich report the indicator rolls up intoWhat the indicator is attached to, and therefore who collects it

They vary independently, which is exactly why both columns are needed. An Institutional KPI — say, overall budget variance — can be tracked at Project level within a specific project. A Project Level Indicator demanded by a donor may be measured at Deliverable level, against the training curriculum that was actually handed over. Knowing one tells you nothing about the other.

In the table, Project Level shows the level as a chip with the thing it is attached to beneath it — the phase, deliverable or task by name. At Project level that second line is suppressed, because it would simply repeat the Project column beside it.

06

Data collection and quality assurance

Data Collection

This is where indicators stop being definitions and start being results. A data record is one indicator's actual value for one reporting period, and it carries the evidence with it.

FieldPurpose
Indicator + IDWhat is being reported, linked back to its definition
ProjectWhich project the result belongs to
Reporting periodThe quarter or period, e.g. "Q1 2025 (Jan–Mar)"
Actual valueWhat was achieved
Target valueWhat was planned, carried across from the indicator
EvidenceFiles or links substantiating the number, with a count shown on the row
ExplanationNarrative — required when performance falls short, see below
QA statusDraft · Pending · Verified · Flagged

Disaggregation

The entry form splits a headline figure into its parts — male and female, urban and rural — alongside the total. This is not decoration: a programme that reaches its overall target while reaching almost no women has not succeeded, and an aggregate number hides that completely. Most donors require disaggregation, and it is far easier to capture at entry than to reconstruct afterwards.

The variance rule

Achievement is computed as the actual against the target:

achievement % = actual ÷ target × 100 below 85% → variance alert raised, explanation required

Under 85 per cent, the form raises an alert and will not treat the record as complete until a variance explanation is written. The completion tracker counts four things — both actual values, evidence or narrative, and a variance explanation where one is owed — so a shortfall cannot be quietly submitted as a bare number. An under-performing indicator with a reason attached is useful; one without is just bad news with no learning in it.

Quality assurance

Draft→Pending→Verified·Flagged

A record entered is Draft; submitted for review it is Pending; checked against its evidence it becomes Verified; where the evidence does not support the number, or the number looks wrong, it is Flagged and goes back. Only verified records should reach a donor report — that is the whole purpose of the status existing.

07

MEL dashboard

Dashboard

Seven configurable panels, each of which can be shown or hidden, filtered by date, project and donor: summary statistics, gender distribution, funding by donor type, indicator achievement trend, grant utilisation, reporting compliance rate, and beneficiary reach. Additional series behind them cover outcome performance and data quality.

The pairing to notice is reporting compliance next to data quality. Compliance says reports went out on time; quality says whether the numbers in them were verified. High compliance with low quality is the failure mode worth catching early, and it is invisible unless both are on the same screen.

08

Reporting and analytics

Reporting & Analytics

Four report areas, each answering to a different audience.

Donor reports

One row per report obligation: the donor, the project, the report type — Quarterly Financial, Quarterly Narrative, Annual Progress, Semi-Annual Progress, Mid-Term Evaluation, Annual Audit Report — the period, the due date, the submitted date, a status of Submitted, In Draft, Due Soon or Overdue, and a compliance verdict of On Time, Late, Pending or Overdue.

Why status and compliance are separate columns

A report can be Submitted and still Late — the fourth example in the seeded data is exactly that, due 30 April and submitted 2 May. Collapsing the two into one column would erase the distinction between did we file it and did we file it on time, which is precisely what a donor relationship turns on.

Management reports

Internal reporting across seven categories — Portfolio, Financial, Operations, Risk, Procurement, Governance — each with a frequency, a period, who generated it (including "System Auto"), a status of Draft or Final, and a trend direction. Burn rate, resource utilisation, risk register, procurement status, cash flow forecast and the quarterly board report all live here.

M&E reports

The M&E function's own reporting, across Performance, Framework, Beneficiary, Data Quality, Learning, Cross-Cutting and Review types. Two columns distinguish these from everything else: a data quality score and the number of indicators tracked. A report covering 45 indicators at 92% quality and one covering 6 at 85% are very different objects, and both numbers are needed to tell them apart.

Impact assessment

Formal evaluations, each with a methodology, a scope, a date range, a beneficiary count, a status, and an impact score. Seven methodologies are represented, and the choice among them is a real methodological decision:

MethodologyHow it establishes attribution
RCTRandom assignment to treatment and control. The strongest claim available, and the most demanding to run.
Quasi-ExperimentalA comparison group constructed rather than randomised — used where randomisation is impossible or unethical.
Pre-Post DesignThe same group measured before and after. Simple, and cannot separate the programme's effect from everything else happening at the time.
Mixed MethodsQuantitative measurement plus qualitative enquiry, each covering the other's blind spot.
Contribution AnalysisTests whether the theory of change held, rather than isolating an effect — appropriate where many actors contributed.
Cost-Benefit AnalysisValues outcomes against costs. Answers "was it worth it", not "did it work".

The impact score sits at 0 until an assessment reaches analysis — a study still collecting data has no score, and the table shows that honestly rather than displaying a placeholder figure.

10

What is real, what is presentation

The honest summary

MEL is presentation throughout. Every screen holds its data as a local array inside the component. Nothing written on one screen reaches another, and nothing survives navigating away. No screen in this module imports a store from lib/.

What is genuinely there is the model — the framework hierarchy with parent linking, the four-section indicator definition including the PIRS, disaggregated collection, the QA statuses, and the variance rule. The thinking is sound and the forms are complete. It is the wiring underneath that is absent.

AreaStatus
Framework hierarchy builderReal as an interaction. Levels add, rename and remove; items link to parents; the review step works. Not persisted.
Variance rule (85%)Real. Computed from actual against target, drives the alert and the completion tracker.
Achievement calculationReal. Computed, not typed.
Disaggregation captureReal as a form. The fields exist and are captured; nothing aggregates them afterwards.
Conditional linked-entity fieldReal. Appears only for Program and Project level frameworks.
CSV exportReal. Frameworks and indicators both export what is currently filtered.
QA verificationNot yet. Statuses are displayed and a Verify action exists, but no separation of duty is enforced — nothing stops the person who entered a record verifying it.
PersistenceNot yet. Submitting a framework, an indicator or a data record logs to the console and returns to the list.
Framework → indicator linkNot yet. The indicator form offers a fixed list of frameworks rather than the ones actually created.
Indicator → data → report flowNot yet. Each of the three is seeded independently, so the same indicator's numbers need not agree across them.
Dashboard figuresNot yet. Seeded series, not aggregated from indicators or records.
If this is built out, the order that pays

One store, following the chain. A lib/melStore.ts holding frameworks, indicators and data records — with indicators referencing a framework ID and records referencing an indicator ID — would make the chain in section 1 real rather than described. Two controls then become possible and are worth having early: refuse to verify a record submitted by the same person, which is the control procurement already applies to deliverable acceptance, and compute the dashboard and the M&E reports' data quality score from actual verified records instead of seeding them.

Glossary

BaselineThe value of an indicator before the work started. Without it, an actual has nothing to be compared against.
TargetThe value the indicator is expected to reach by the target date.
ActualWhat was in fact achieved in a reporting period.
AchievementActual as a percentage of target. Below 85%, an explanation is required.
DisaggregationSplitting a figure by group — sex, location — so an aggregate cannot hide who was missed.
PIRSPerformance Indicator Reference Sheet. The definitive statement of how an indicator is measured, so the number means the same thing over time.
Means of verificationWhere the evidence for an indicator comes from. A required column in a logical framework.
AssumptionSomething that must hold true for the results chain to work, but which is outside our control. Named explicitly in a theory of change and a logframe.
Output vs outcomeAn output is what we produced — a training hub built. An outcome is what changed as a result — people gaining skills. Outputs are within our control; outcomes are not, which is why both are measured.
Attribution vs contributionAttribution claims our programme caused the change. Contribution claims we helped. Where many actors are involved, contribution is the honest claim, and contribution analysis is how it is tested.
Outcome harvestingWorking backwards from observed changes to what caused them, rather than checking pre-set indicators. Useful where outcomes could not be predicted in advance.
